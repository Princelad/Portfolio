From Desktop Comfort to i3 Mastery: A CS Student's Linux Evolution

Mar 19, 2026

1. Introduction

I did not switch to i3 because it looked cool in screenshots. I switched because I was tired of losing time in small, invisible ways. Traditional desktop environments were polished, but they often felt like they were designed for broad convenience, not for the pace and focus I needed as a B.Tech Computer Science student in India who spends most of the day coding, reading docs, and moving between terminals.

At first, my frustration sounded minor: too many clicks, too many floating windows, too much visual noise, too many context switches that broke concentration. But over months, those small frictions became a bigger question: do I want to consume a desktop environment, or build one around how I actually think?

That curiosity led me to tiling window managers, and eventually to i3. What started as a setup experiment turned into something much deeper. i3 did not just change my window layout. It changed the way I approach systems, problems, and even learning itself.

2. Why I Moved Away from Traditional DEs

Before i3, I used more traditional environments while moving across distros, including Fedora and CachyOS, and then settling into an Arch-based workflow. KDE and other full desktop setups gave me a lot out of the box, and for many users that is exactly what matters. But for my workflow, three limitations kept repeating.

First was resource behavior. Modern DEs are capable, but they carry background processes and visual layers that I did not always need. On a coding-heavy day with browsers, language servers, containers, and local databases running, I wanted every bit of system focus to go toward development.

Second was mouse dependency. I can type quickly, think quickly, and navigate code quickly. Needing the mouse for window placement, workspace handling, and application switching started to feel like an unnecessary tax.

Third was control. Desktop environments often expose customization, but within pre-defined boundaries. I wanted direct control over behavior, not just themes and panel widgets. At some point, comfort becomes a soft trap. You stay productive enough to avoid changing, but never optimized enough to feel in complete command.

3. First Encounter with i3 (Reality Check)

My first i3 session was humbling. I logged in and saw what looked like a nearly blank screen. No familiar panel setup, no obvious launcher, no visual hints about what to do next. It felt less like entering a new desktop and more like opening an empty editor with no keymap.

The keybindings felt foreign. I had to remember the Mod key combinations, understand splitting modes, learn workspace jumps, and keep the i3 user guide open in another tab. What I expected was immediate speed. What I got was friction.

But the emotional curve was interesting: confusion came first, then curiosity. Curiosity became determination when I realized every annoyance was also a path to understanding. The blankness was not a missing feature. It was an invitation to define behavior deliberately.

4. The Configuration Phase (Core of the Journey)

This was the phase that truly changed me. I stopped asking, "Which setting should I click?" and started asking, "What exact behavior do I want?" Most of that work lived in ~/.config/i3/config, where each line had a direct relationship with my day-to-day workflow.

I chose a Mod key strategy that matched my muscle memory and built a keybinding system around movement, app launching, and workspace discipline. Instead of opening windows randomly and fixing layout later, I started assigning tasks to dedicated workspaces: coding, docs, browser research, and communication.

I also integrated tools that made i3 feel complete without sacrificing minimalism. Rofi became my fast, keyboard-first app launcher. Picom handled compositing where needed. Dunst gave me clean notifications without interrupting flow. i3blocks provided a status bar that told me exactly what I cared about, and nothing more.

Manual features taught me the most. Projector setup over HDMI was not always plug-and-play, so I wrote small xrandr scripts for predictable layouts during presentations and lab work. Media keys did not work magically in early setups, so I mapped them explicitly and connected them to pactl/playerctl commands. These small scripts looked simple, but they gave me a stronger system intuition than any GUI wizard ever had.

A simplified excerpt from my i3 config looked like this: 

set $mod Mod4

bindsym $mod+Return exec alacritty
bindsym $mod+d exec rofi -show drun
bindsym $mod+Shift+r restart

# Workspace routing
set $ws1 "1:code"
set $ws2 "2:web"
set $ws3 "3:sys"
bindsym $mod+1 workspace $ws1
bindsym $mod+2 workspace $ws2
bindsym $mod+3 workspace $ws3

# Hardware keys
bindsym XF86AudioRaiseVolume exec pactl set-sink-volume @DEFAULT_SINK@ +5%
bindsym XF86AudioLowerVolume exec pactl set-sink-volume @DEFAULT_SINK@ -5%
bindsym XF86AudioMute exec pactl set-sink-mute @DEFAULT_SINK@ toggle

That code is not extraordinary, but the process behind it was. Every tweak came from observing pain, testing a fix, breaking something, reading documentation, and trying again. i3 rewarded learning by doing in the purest way.

5. Workflow Transformation

Before i3, my workflow was reactive. I opened apps first and organized later. Windows overlapped, focus drifted, and context switching often meant searching for what I had lost on the screen.

After i3, the workflow became intentional. Navigation is almost entirely keyboard-driven. Workspaces are predictable. Context switches are faster because they are named and mapped in muscle memory. I move from writing code in Alacritty to checking docs to running tests with near-zero cognitive interruption.

The biggest gain is not raw speed; it is consistency. A predictable interface removes decision fatigue. For developers, that matters more than flashy UI. In my view, tiling WMs are superior for serious coding not because they are trendy, but because they align the operating environment with how engineering work actually happens: parallel tasks, fast switching, and long concentration windows.

6. Challenges and Friction Points

i3 is powerful, but it is honest about its trade-offs. Multi-monitor behavior and HDMI projector setups needed extra scripting and testing, especially across different machines or classroom displays. Media keys were inconsistent until I explicitly configured them. Many conveniences that are automatic in DEs are manual here.

Configuration errors were another reality. A single typo in the config can lead to confusing behavior, and debugging takes patience. I had to learn to reload safely, keep backups, and validate one change at a time.

Most importantly, i3 requires time. If someone expects immediate polish without setup effort, the experience can feel punishing. The payoff is real, but the initial investment is non-trivial.

7. What i3 Taught Me

This is the most meaningful part of the journey. i3 shifted my identity from being only a user of Linux to becoming a builder of my own environment.

I began to understand Linux internals with more practical depth: how sessions start, how key events are handled, how display configuration works, and how compositors and notification daemons fit into the overall system. I read more documentation than ever before, and I became more comfortable debugging from first principles instead of searching only for copy-paste fixes.

I also started writing small utility scripts more naturally. Whether it was monitor profiles, startup routines, or quick automation hooks, I learned to treat script-writing as an everyday engineering skill, not a special event. That habit has improved how I approach all technical problems, even outside Linux customization.

8. My Current Setup

Today, my setup is intentionally simple and deeply personal. I run an Arch-based Linux system with i3 as my primary window manager. Alacritty is my terminal, zsh is my shell, and Gruvbox defines the visual language across terminal, editor, and status components.

Around that core, I use rofi, dunst, i3blocks, and a few custom scripts tailored to my student and developer workflow. The setup is not built to impress anyone. It reflects how I think, how I work, and what I value: speed, clarity, and control.

9. i3 vs Hyprland

I also use Hyprland in some contexts, and comparing both helped me understand my own priorities better. Hyprland is visually modern, smooth, and aesthetically engaging. It brings animation and a different kind of desktop feel that many users love.

i3, however, remains my default for focused development. It is stable, straightforward, and brutally predictable. If I need a system that behaves exactly the same way every day under heavy development load, i3 still wins for me. If I want a more dynamic visual experience and Wayland-native polish, Hyprland is compelling.

10. Who Should (and Should Not) Use i3

i3 is ideal for developers, power users, and students who want to understand Linux deeply while building efficient habits. If you value keyboard-first navigation and are willing to invest in configuration, the long-term return is excellent.

It is less suitable for users who expect plug-and-play convenience or who dislike editing configs and troubleshooting system behavior. There is nothing wrong with choosing comfort-first tools. But i3 is best when you are ready to trade convenience for control.

Tips for Beginners

If you are starting with i3, begin small. Keep default settings for a few days, then change only one thing at a time. Document your keybindings, version your dotfiles, and keep a fallback session available. Learn rofi early, script monitor profiles before you need them, and treat the official documentation as your primary mentor.

11. Conclusion

Looking back, i3 changed more than my productivity metrics. It changed my thinking style. I now approach systems with a builder's mindset: observe friction, inspect internals, automate intentionally, and refine continuously.

As a Computer Science student and Linux enthusiast, that shift has been one of the most valuable parts of my technical journey. i3 is not just a window manager. For me, it became a mindset transition from consuming tools to shaping them.
